Ask ten enthusiasts to name an icon and you will hear the same handful of cars. Few of them were the fastest of their generation. Almost none were the most expensive. What they share is harder to measure: a single idea, executed without dilution.

## One idea, fully resolved

The cars we remember tend to be answers to a clear question. How light can a road car be? How much drama fits in a mid-engine silhouette? What does a grand tourer feel like if nothing is allowed to rattle? When the question is sharp, every other decision — the seating position, the weight of the steering, the sound at idle — follows from it.

Cars designed by committee rarely become icons. They are competent at everything and memorable for nothing.

## The moment matters

Timing plays a part. The last naturally aspirated engine in a line, the final manual gearbox, the last model before a regulation changed everything: these become reference points precisely because nothing came after them. The market understands this very quickly.

## Proportion before detail

Stand twenty metres away from an iconic car and you still recognise it. Proportion does the work — the relationship between wheel and body, the length of the bonnet, where the cabin sits. Details are wonderful up close, but they cannot rescue a shape that is wrong.

## What this means when you buy

We look for cars where the original idea is still intact: unmodified where it matters, maintained rather than restored, specified by someone who understood what the car was for. Those are the examples that stay interesting — to drive, and to own.
